3. Once a Benford, always a Benford

Multiply every number by a constant (a change of units, currency or "in thousands") and re-test the first digit. Real Benford data keeps its shape. Data that only looks right by accident does not.

5. Why growth makes Benford

A quantity grows at r% a year from 1,000 to 10,000. How long does it spend with each leading digit? Years with first digit d = log(1 + 1/d) / log(1 + r). As a share of the whole trip that is log10(1 + 1/d), whatever r is. This is the same calculation as NPER in Excel.

Teaching notes: where this comes from

Newcomb, 1881. Simon Newcomb noticed that the early pages of books of logarithm tables (numbers starting with 1) were more worn than the later ones, and proposed that the probability of a leading digit d is log10(1 + 1/d).

Benford, 1938. Frank Benford, a General Electric physicist, rediscovered it and tested 20,229 observations from 20 different datasets (rivers, populations, addresses, physical constants and more).

Scale invariance. Pinkham (1961) showed that if a digit law does not depend on the units of measurement, it must be the logarithmic law. That is what section 3 shows.

Forensic accounting. Mark Nigrini popularised digit tests for auditors in the 1990s. They work on data spanning several orders of magnitude and not constrained by caps or assigned numbers (phone numbers, check numbers). A deviation is a reason to look closer, not proof of fraud.
